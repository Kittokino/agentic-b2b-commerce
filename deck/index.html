This deck explores how an AI agent could handle B2B orders in the channels people already use, without taking decisions away from the people who own them.

It follows one order from a chef's voice note to a confirmed delivery, showing where the agent acts, where it stops to ask, and where it hands over to a person.

All companies, people and figures are fictional.

The problem

25 – 30%

of B2B transactions are digital.
The rest still runs through people and messages.

Source: Sana case brief

How might we let buyers order in the channels they already use, with an agent that acts on their behalf, and knows when to stop?

Most B2B orders still arrive as messages, calls and emails, in people's own words. An agent can turn those into orders and keep every conversation tied to its order, but every order spends someone else's money.

So the question isn't just how to automate ordering. It's how to let the agent act where that's safe, and pause for the right person where it isn't. Seamless doesn't mean never pausing; sometimes the pause is the design.

The places & the people

The Olive Tree Restaurant chain

Kade Foodservice Wholesaler · Runs on Sana

The Olive Tree is a restaurant chain with three outlets. Priya, the central buyer, buys for all of them: she wants to never run out, waste less and stay within budget. Nigel, head chef at the Harbour outlet, orders within Priya's rules, usually by WhatsApp after service.

Kade Foodservice is a food & beverage wholesaler running on Sana. Its agent takes orders on every channel, within rules set by Maheep, the admin. Tom, a service rep, takes over when the agent runs out of options.

In this story, people still place and approve the orders. The agent works for the wholesaler and helps them; agents ordering on a buyer's behalf come later, as a vision.

The people come first; the agent comes last, shown as a symbol rather than a face. It never presents itself as a person.

Hands over

Each decision goes to the person who owns it.

Each line of the order ends differently. The cheese board is within budget, so the agent confirms it on its own. The salmon would take Harbour over its fish budget, so the agent stops and asks Priya, without changing the order. The Sauvignon Blanc is low on stock, so the agent suggests a Pinot Grigio; Nigel declines because he needs Sauvignon Blanc for the food pairing, and the agent hands the case to Tom.

In short: budget goes to Priya, taste to Nigel, the rest to the agent. These are the same verbs as on the title slide: acts, stops, hands over.

    Every channel

The same order over two days and three channels: WhatsApp for Nigel, Teams for Priya, and Kade's service desk for Tom. Each step shows who is involved and what they want at that moment. The agent has a rule instead of a thought: it confirms what it may, and asks about what it may not. The red dot marks the moment it stops.

Priya gets one card in Teams showing all three lines. Only the salmon needs her decision; the rest is for information. When Nigel declines the substitute, her card updates quietly: "Wine: with Tom at Kade from 08:00."

Early on Thursday, Priya's own assistant checks stock across her outlets and finds ten bottles at Old Town. Priya calls Old Town to check they can spare them: asking a colleague to give up stock is a conversation between people. She adjusts the wine line in her Teams card: "10 from Old Town. Source 14." She does this before Kade's service desk opens, so the agent updates the order and Tom's case says 14, not 24. Nothing is over-ordered, and nothing needs refunding.

Tom finds the 14 bottles at a partner supplier and sends a one-off quote, not a contract change. Priya approves the salmon, Nigel confirms the wine, and the order is confirmed: the same in WhatsApp, Teams and Kade's webshop. In the other branch, Nigel accepts the Pinot Grigio and no hand-over is needed; the prototype lets you try both paths.

Assumptions & research

What we assume, and how we'd find out

What we assume

  1. Buyers let the agent confirm orders within their rules
  2. Chefs order by voice note and WhatsApp
  3. Chefs accept a suggested swap for taste items
  4. Spend is controlled by category budgets per outlet
  5. Buyers want quiet updates for things they don't decide
  6. Buyers are happy to approve in Teams

The design rests on six assumptions that have not been tested yet. The three filled ones are tested first, because they would matter most if wrong and there is little evidence today: whether buyers will let an agent confirm orders within their rules, whether chefs really order by voice note and WhatsApp, and whether chefs accept a suggested substitute for items chosen on taste.

Before launch: an evening observing a kitchen after service, an analysis of how Kade's orders arrive today, interviews with buyers, chefs and service reps, and a Wizard of Oz test in which a person plays the agent on WhatsApp for two weeks across three outlets. After launch: how much the agent handles alone, how often people override it or need a hand-over, a one-tap trust check after decisions, and a four-week diary study.
