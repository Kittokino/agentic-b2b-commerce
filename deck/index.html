This deck looks at how an AI agent could take care of B2B orders in the channels people already use, while the decisions stay with the people who own them.

It follows one order, from a chef's voice note to a confirmed delivery, and shows where the agent acts on its own, where it stops to ask, and where it hands over to a person.

All companies, people and figures are fictional.

The problem

25 – 30%

of B2B transactions are digital.
The rest still runs through people and messages.

Source: Sana case brief

How might we let buyers order in the channels they already use, with an agent that acts on their behalf, and knows when to stop?

Most B2B orders still arrive as messages, calls and emails, in people's own words. An agent can turn these into orders and keep all related conversations attached to the order.

In B2B, the person placing an order is often not the person who controls the budget. In a restaurant chain, for example, a head chef orders and a central buyer approves spending above the restaurant's limits. The agent therefore needs to know when it can act on its own, and when it should ask someone else.

The problem and the principles

The problem I chose, and four principles for solving it

How do restaurants order their supplies from a wholesaler? My desk research into this, together with my own experience, led me to these four principles.

  1. The order lives in one record.

    This comes from my work with Salesforce. I have worked on workflows that bring an AI agent into a process, where messages from different channels are tied back to one order until it is fulfilled.

  2. The agent works within rules that people set, and people improve the rules.

    This comes from following how AI agents are built today. Agents can be granted access, assigned limits and improved through regular testing, and every action they take can be recorded and undone.

  3. People only see what needs a decision.

    This comes from experience of how people work. Everyone's time is limited, so a person should only be asked when a decision is theirs to make.

  4. Every decision goes to the person who owns it, and affects only the item it is about.

    This comes from experience of how teams work. People hold roles that come with responsibility and accountability, so each decision has to move along the chain to the person who is accountable for it.

I narrowed B2B ordering down to one problem, so that the design could be concrete. These four principles guide everything that follows, and each of them appears in the story.

I have seen the first principle work in my own projects. The third and fourth are true of any team, so research would not test whether they are true. It would test how they apply here, for example who decides what, and above which amount.

The places & the people

The Olive Tree Restaurant chain

Kade Foodservice Wholesaler · Runs on Sana

The Olive Tree is a restaurant chain with three outlets. Priya is the central buyer for all of them. She wants to avoid running out of stock, keep waste low and stay within budget. Nigel is the head chef at the Harbour Front outlet. He orders within Priya's rules, mostly by WhatsApp after service.

Hands over

Each decision goes to the person who owns it.

The agent works for Kade, within the rules set by Maheep. It always stays within each buyer's contract, meaning the agreed products and prices.

What it does: it turns orders from any channel into order lines, checks them against the contract and the buyer's budgets, reads them back, and keeps all related conversations attached to the order.

What it aims for: fewer orders typed in by hand, quicker confirmations, and each decision taken by the person responsible for it.

What it never does: go outside the contract, go over a budget, swap an item without asking, change an order without saying so, or present itself as a person. When it stops, it explains why. When it can't go further, it hands over to a person with all the context.

Configuring the platform

One platform, set up with different rules

Every industry needs the same kinds of rules. Only the settings change. Maheep owns each rule, tests every change before publishing it, and can go back to an earlier version.

Kade FoodserviceA medical supplier
SubstitutesThe agent suggests up to three alternatives, and the person who ordered decides.The agent never suggests a substitute for a medicine. A pharmacist decides.
ApprovalsThe buyer approves anything over an outlet's budget.Prescription-only medicines need a licence on file.
PricingStaples have contract prices, and fresh items like fish change with the market. A one-time price goes to the buyer.Prices are fixed for the length of each hospital's agreement, and in many countries the law caps medicine prices.
RecordsEach decision records the rule and the version it followed.Each item also records its lot number, so a recall can be traced.
DeliveryOrders go on Kade's usual delivery routes.Items that must stay cold go only on refrigerated routes.

Note: Today, setting this up often needs developers. I would work with engineering and product to make it simple for any admin.

Maheep, Kade's admin, sets the rules the agent follows. A medical supplier would use the same kinds of rules with stricter settings, because medicines carry more risk.

I would shape these rules together with engineering and product, because making them easy to set up, test and undo is not something a designer solves alone.

Design system

One order, drawn in every channel

I started from the principles of Microsoft's Fluent design system, because Teams is one of the channels and buyers work in Microsoft 365. On top of that sits one small set of colours, type and components, which the deck and the prototype share.

Note: The whole system is written down in DESIGN.md, so an AI tool can show the same order in a new channel, such as email.

The salmon is the same item in all three places, with the same status words and the same red for the agent's stop. Only the form changes: a message, a card and an activity entry. The status label adapts to who is looking, so Priya reads "Awaiting approval" while everyone else reads "Waiting on Priya".

Assumptions & research

What I assume, and how I would find out

My assumptions

  1. Buyers let the agent confirm orders within their rules
  2. Chefs order by voice note and WhatsApp
  3. Chefs accept a suggested swap for taste items
  4. Spend is controlled by category budgets per outlet
  5. Buyers want quiet updates for things they don't decide
  6. Buyers are happy to approve in Teams

Test these first, because they cost the most if wrong.

How I would find out

I would follow how each person spends their day, look at the tools they use and why they have not learnt more of them, spend time where they work, and talk to them about their needs and their hardest challenges.

Then test, and measure

First, a person would play the agent on WhatsApp, so we learn whether people accept it. After launch, I would measure how much the agent handles on its own, how often people overrule it, and whether they trust it.

The first three assumptions would hurt the design most if they turn out wrong, so I would test them first. I would start with the steps I use for any new problem, then let a person play the agent, and measure once it is live.

If asked for numbers: a day in the life with six people (two chefs, two buyers, two service reps), four days where they work, six to eight interviews, then two weeks of Wizard of Oz across three outlets, and a four-week diary study after launch.

Vision

Trust earns the next step.

Commerce is going headless. Agents will order from agents, and people will do their part from wherever they work, connected to their own data, without having to learn yet another tool.

Agents will anticipate. They will read the weather, market movements and the news, and act for the business they work for before anyone asks. Makers, wholesalers and logistics will be connected end to end, so food stays fresh, prices stay steady, and far less goes to waste.

And people get their time back, for the craft and for each other.

I believe commerce is going headless, something I came to believe in at Salesforce. Agents do the work, and people do not need a particular screen to follow it. Trust sets the pace. Each step forward is earned, every action stays recorded, and people can undo it.

Three things stand in the way. People fear becoming obsolete. Systems must be connected safely, while each business keeps control of its data. And it all costs money, energy and people's effort. I believe the fear will pass. When AI takes the routine work, people rediscover the joy of working together and learning the craft from one another.
